Our simulated "handler" is the if (pressed != last_pressed) block inside the loop, which triggers only when a real change happens:
Inside this block, we check if (pressed) to see if the button just went down. If it did, we add 1 to the rising_edge counter.

## GPIO: Simple Outputs

Q: Justify the circuit and it's impact on the power/voltage/current limits of the Pico.  That is, provide clear evidence/proof that the way the LED is connected is within the limits of the Pico.
The Pico's GPIO pins output 3.3V. A standard red LED typically requires a forward voltage of about 1.8V to 2.0V to turn on. According to Ohm's law (V = I * R), we can calculate the current. The voltage dropping across our provided 220 Ohm resistor is the remaining voltage: 3.3V - 1.8V = 1.5V.
So, 1.5V / 220Ohm = ~0.0068 Amps, or 6.8 milliamps (mA).
The RP2350 datasheet shows that a single GPIO pin can safely source around 12mA of current. 

Q: Imagine you want to connect more LEDs.  Will power/voltage/current limits be an issue?  If so, how may you overcome any limitations?
Yes, it will definitely be an issue. While one pin can handle 6.8mA, the Pico's chip has a total maximum current limit for all GPIO pins combined (often around 50mA total).
To overcome this, we can use a transistor (like a MOSFET). Instead of powering the LEDs directly from the GPIO pins, we would connect the LEDs directly to the main power supply through the transistor. The Pico's GPIO pin would only need to send a tiny, low-current signal to the transistor to flip it "on" or "off" like a switch
